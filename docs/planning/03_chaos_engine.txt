======================================================================
1. WHAT "CHAOS" MEANS                                     [ACCEPTED]
======================================================================

Chaos measures how unreliable and disruptive the system is for
passengers, not how many things are going wrong.

  - Ten minor delays should not automatically outweigh one suspension.
  - Fewer trains at midnight is not chaos if that is the scheduled
    service.
  - Missing data is not good service.


The scoring method requires research and empirical validation. All candidate weights remain provisional.

======================================================================
2. HOW THE MODEL EVOLVED
======================================================================

Candidate A - early illustrative simulator                 [MOCK]

    Factor                       Weight   Simulator value
    Disruption severity            35%       40/100
    Affected network coverage      20%       25/100
    Measurable delays              25%       30/100
    Service suspensions            20%        0/100
    Displayed result: 27/100, labelled "Moderate chaos"
    (0.35x40 + 0.20x25 + 0.25x30 + 0.20x0 = 26.5)

  Purpose: show what an explainable score could look like.
  Weakness: almost entirely alert-driven; coverage and severity overlap;
  "measurable delays" undefined.
  Note: its "Moderate" label at 27 disagrees with the later bands below
  (20-39 = "Minor chaos"). Severity labels remain open.

Candidate B - five pillars                                 [PROPOSED]

    Pillar                 Weight  Measures
    Service disruptions      30%   suspensions, closures, shuttles, detours
    Schedule deviation       25%   predicted/observed vs scheduled
    Service frequency        20%   long gaps and bunching
    Disruption coverage      15%   share of network/route affected
    Service availability     10%   expected service actually operating

  Simulator example [MOCK]: 40, 30, 50, 20, 10 -> about 34/100.
  Change from A: moves from "count alerts" to "measure service the
  passenger receives", and introduces time-of-day expectations and the
  idea that bunching can be chaotic without any official alert.
  Disruption type ranking proposed: suspension (critical) > station
  closure (very high) > shuttle / significant delay (high) > detour
  (moderate-high) > minor delay (low) > informational (minimal).
  Planned future disruptions do not count until active.

Candidate C - research-informed starting proposal          [PROPOSED]
  Candidate weights require calibration against passenger outcomes.

    Factor                     Weight
    Excess waiting time          30%
    Service disruptions          25%
    Excess travel time           20%
    Service unpredictability     15%
    Service availability         10%

  Disruption coverage is removed as a weighted factor. It becomes an
  exposure modifier: it scales how much an individual disruption
  contributes to a route or network score. This reduces double
  counting (a suspension is already "severe"; counting its coverage
  separately counts it twice).
  Change from B: reframes the core around time lost by passengers
  (waiting and riding), which is what both the MBTA's Excess Trip Time
  metric and Furth & Muller's work measure.

Factor definitions for candidate C [PROPOSED; measurability per input
UNVALIDATED]. Each factor is normalized to 0-100 before weighting.

  Excess waiting     extra waiting vs expected service. Inputs: scheduled
                     and observed headways, headway variability, gaps,
                     missing departures, prediction changes.
  Service disruptions impact of suspensions, partial closures, shuttles,
                     station closures, detours, significant delays,
                     accessibility disruptions. Alert count alone is not
                     a measure.
  Excess travel time extra in-vehicle or journey time vs expected.
                     Inputs: scheduled and typical travel time, observed
                     movement, long dwells, segment delays.
  Unpredictability   how hard service is to anticipate. Inputs: headway
                     variability, prediction revisions and errors,
                     irregular spacing, unexpected service changes.
  Availability       whether expected service is running. Inputs:
                     expected vs observed trips, suspended segments,
                     frequency reductions, geographic gaps.

  Conceptual combination (candidate C, not validated):
    score = 0.30 W + 0.25 D + 0.20 T + 0.15 U + 0.10 A
  Note the overlap: headway variability feeds both W and U, and one
  incident can raise all five. Section 5 (correlated evidence) applies.

Why it evolved, in one line: from a demo of explainability (A), to a
passenger-service view (B), to a time-lost view grounded in published
reliability measures (C).

======================================================================
3. HOW TO COMPARE CANDIDATES                              [PROPOSED]
======================================================================

  1. Freeze each candidate as a versioned model (A, B, C, later ones).
  2. Replay the same historical periods through all of them.
  3. Compare against a passenger-relevant outcome measured
     independently of the score (for example observed excess wait or
     excess trip time for the same scope and period).
  4. Report agreement (rank correlation, error by band), behaviour on
     the scenario tests in section 9, and sensitivity (section 8).
  5. Prefer the simpler model when performance is similar.
  A candidate cannot be compared until history exists. Until then the
  live score must be labelled experimental.

======================================================================
4. SCOPES                                                 [ACCEPTED]
======================================================================

  NETWORK   whole MBTA (or the modes in scope)
  ROUTE     one route or branch, optionally one direction
  COMMUTE   one saved journey: boarding stop, destination, direction,
            time window, transfers, optional accessibility needs

  [PROPOSED] Compute route scores first; build network from routes
  weighted by expected service/importance; build commute scores from
  the journey's own segments.

  Network aggregation may consider [UNVALIDATED]: expected service,
  route importance, passenger exposure where reliable data exists,
  geographic scope, time of day, direction, branch, available
  alternatives. Coverage acts through aggregation, not as its own
  factor.

  A critical route-level event must make that route critical. It must
  not by itself make the whole network critical. [ACCEPTED principle;
  method UNVALIDATED]

  Illustrative contrast [MOCK]:
    BU Central -> Park Street (Green B), normal service ........ 12
    Harvard -> Alewife (Red), suspension on the journey ........ 92
    Central -> Kendall (Red), outside suspended section ........ 34
  Same line, very different personal impact.

5. RULES THE ENGINE MUST FOLLOW
======================================================================

  Rule                          Status       Behaviour
  ----------------------------  -----------  ------------------------------
  Severe suspension handling    [ACCEPTED]   An active suspension in scope
                                             sets a minimum score/band; normal
                                             values elsewhere cannot hide it.
                                             Floor value [OPEN].
  Time-of-day expectations      [ACCEPTED]   Compare against scheduled
                                             service for that time and day.
  Mode-specific expectations    [ACCEPTED]   Subway headway logic is not
                                             applied to timetable service.
  Direction awareness           [ACCEPTED]   Inbound problems do not raise
                                             an outbound commute score.
  Geographic awareness          [ACCEPTED]   Beyond-destination problems
                                             count as indirect at most.
  Correlated evidence           [ACCEPTED]   One incident seen as an alert,
                                             a gap, and missing departures
                                             counts once at full weight.
  Duplicate alerts              [ACCEPTED]   Several alerts for one incident
                                             do not inflate the score.
  Gradual recovery              [ACCEPTED]   Short fluctuations do not swing
                                             the score. Whether a new severe
                                             event bypasses smoothing [OPEN].
  Confidence separate from      [ACCEPTED]   A high score can be low
  severity                                   confidence and vice versa.
  Explainability                [ACCEPTED]   Every score lists its factors.

  Not direct inputs without context [PROPOSED]: raw vehicle counts, raw
  alert counts, speed alone, weather alone, traffic alone, AI-generated
  judgements. The engine should be deterministic and testable.

======================================================================
6. MISSING DATA                                           [ACCEPTED]
======================================================================

  - A missing feed never becomes a healthy zero.
  - Missing factor weights are never silently redistributed to the
    remaining factors.
  - Each factor reports: available | partial | unavailable.
  - If enough evidence remains, return a PROVISIONAL score with reduced
    confidence and name the missing factors. Otherwise return UNKNOWN.
  - The threshold for "enough evidence" is [OPEN]; it must be stated in
    the model version, not hidden.

  Example [MOCK]: predictions lost
    Excess waiting ........ unavailable
    Disruptions ........... available
    Excess travel time .... unavailable
    Unpredictability ...... partial
    Availability .......... available
    -> provisional or unknown, never "smooth".

    0-19   Smooth          service close to expectations
    20-39  Minor chaos     some disruption, generally reliable
    40-59  Moderate chaos  noticeable degradation
    60-79  High chaos      significant travel disruption
    80-100 Critical chaos  severe service failure
  Final wording and thresholds require validation.

  What a rider can see behind any result                   [ACCEPTED]
    - score and band (or UNKNOWN)
    - scope (network / route+direction / commute)
    - contributing factors with their values and status
    - observation time of the newest input and score computation time
    - confidence level and why
    - limitations (e.g. "no prediction data for 6 min")
    - model version (e.g. "candidate C, experimental")
  [PROPOSED] A "How we measure chaos" page with method, formulas,
  sources, and limitations.

======================================================================
8. RESEARCH PROCESS                                       [PROPOSED]
======================================================================

  1. Verify literature: confirm each source exists and says what we
     cite it for.
  2. Define measurable features from data we can actually obtain
     (V3 API now; historical sets if compatible).
  3. Collect historical observations continuously (07).
  4. Calibrate against passenger-relevant outcomes.
  5. Validate on time periods not used for calibration.
  6. Test sensitivity: small weight or input changes should not cause
     unreasonable jumps.
  7. Publish a documented, versioned model.

  Questions the evaluation must answer: do severe disruptions score
  high and normal service low; does the score follow passenger impact;
  do correlated factors exaggerate incidents; is it stable under small
  data fluctuations; do modes need separate normalization; does missing
  data mislead; does it generalize to unseen periods.

  A model is not called "validated" until its factor definitions are
  documented, data sources verified, weights have a defensible basis,
  behaviour is checked against real observations, sensitivity is
  examined, limitations are written down, and it is tested on held-out
  data. The same standard applies to anomaly detection and any
  independent prediction model.

10. WAITING TIME UNDER IRREGULAR HEADWAYS
======================================================================

  E[W] = E[H] x (1 + CV(H)^2) / 2

  H       time between consecutive vehicles at a stop (minutes)
  E[H]    mean headway (minutes)
  CV(H)   coefficient of variation of H = standard deviation / mean
          (dimensionless)
  E[W]    expected passenger wait (minutes)
  Assumption: passengers arrive uniformly at random, independent of the
  timetable. Then E[W] = E[H^2] / (2 E[H]), which rearranges to the
  formula above. Perfectly regular service (CV = 0) gives E[H]/2.

  Simulated example [MOCK]: E[H] = 6 min, CV = 0.8
    regular service wait 3.0 min; with irregularity 3.0 x 1.64 = 4.9 min
    (+64%). Not an observed MBTA value.

  Where it does not fit: timetable-based service (commuter rail, ferry,
  long-headway bus). Riders time their arrival to the schedule, so wait
  depends on schedule deviation, not on headway variance. Furth & Muller
  make this distinction: for long headways, actual and budgeted waits
  relate to the extremes of the schedule-deviation distribution. The
  headway threshold at which to switch models is [OPEN].

  Furth and Muller (2006), equation 2, gives the mean-wait relationship.

11. SOURCES
======================================================================

  Verified (checked November 24, 2026):

  - Furth, P. G. and Muller, T. H. J. (2006). "Service Reliability and
    Hidden Waiting Time: Insights from Automatic Vehicle Location Data."
    Transportation Research Record 1955, 79-87.
    DOI: 10.1177/0361198106195500110.
    Supports: traditional measures underestimate the cost of waiting;
    passengers budget waiting time from an extreme of the wait
    distribution (e.g. 95th percentile); short- vs long-headway waiting
    behaves differently.
    Does not support: any specific EnRoute weight.

  - MBTA OPMI, "Introducing Excess Trip Time" (December 19, 2024).
    ETT = (actual wait + actual travel) - (baseline wait + baseline
    travel), passenger-weighted, for heavy rail (Red, Orange, Blue)
    under the December 2024 Service Delivery Policy. Actual wait is
    headway/2 per trip under a random-arrival assumption; passenger
    loads and wait come from an origin-destination model built on fare
    card validations, which the V3 API does not expose. The dashboard
    shows the share of riders within 5 and within 20 minutes of expected
    trip time; OPMI treats 5 minutes as the primary target.
    Use: an AGENCY BENCHMARK for validating our outcome measure. It is
    not a EnRoute scoring threshold, and "within 5 minutes" is not a
    band boundary.

  - MBTA transit-performance (github.com/mbta/transit-performance).
    MIT-licensed, archived March 4, 2026. Computes headways, travel
    times, schedule adherence, passenger-weighted wait and travel times,
    dwell times, and prediction accuracy from GTFS / GTFS-realtime.
    Use: methodology reference. Not a dependency (archived).

  - MBTA V3 API (api-v3.mbta.com). Source of live inputs.
    /stop_events ("actual arrival and departure times") may supply
    observed arrivals; subway coverage and latency are [UNVALIDATED].

12. ACCEPTANCE CRITERIA FOR ANY SHIPPED SCORE
======================================================================

  - With the alerts feed disconnected, no score shows "Smooth"; the UI
    shows UNKNOWN or PROVISIONAL with the missing factor named.
  - With an active suspension on route R, route R's band is at or above
    the agreed floor regardless of other inputs.
  - Three alerts sharing one incident produce the same score as one.
  - Every displayed score opens an explanation listing scope, factors,
    observation time, confidence, and model version.
  - The score is labelled experimental until section 8 steps 4-6 are
    done.
